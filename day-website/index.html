2400 Baud Modem

The 2400 Baud Modem

Before broadband, before dial-up at 56k, there was the 2400 baud modem. 2400 baud meant 2400 bits per second — roughly 240 characters per second, about as fast as a person can read out loud. A page of text took seconds. A file took minutes. A big file took a phone call you did not want to pay for.

The 2400 baud modem spoke V.22bis. You dialed with Hayes AT commands: ATDT and the number, then the handshake — the clicks, the tones, the hiss settling into carrier. When it connected, the modem printed CONNECT 2400 and you were in.

We used a Commodore 64. The modem plugged into the user port or a cartridge interface, and the terminal program turned the TV into a window onto another computer. 2400 baud felt fast after 300. It felt like the future. It was the speed at which the BBS world ran, and the BBS world was the whole online world we had.

Bbs Culture

BBS Culture

BBS culture was small and it was personal. A board had a sysop you knew by handle, regulars you recognized by their writing, and rules that were enforced by the sysop's delete key. There were no algorithms. The front page was whatever the sysop put there. The new messages were the ones people had left since you last called.

FidoNet was the network of boards. CBBS, the first BBS, was launched in 1978 by Ward Christensen and Randy Suess in Chicago. FidoNet started operating in 1984 and at its peak had over 40,000 nodes. Messages traveled as echomail, files as file echoes, and the whole thing ran on phone calls and trust.

The culture was renegade and it was generous. People shared files, wrote doors, ran boards at a loss because the board was the point. That is the culture CETI comes from: a BBS of brains, signed knowledge passed node to node, your iron, your data, your mind.

Before The 386

Before the 386DX40

All of this — the Commodore 64, the 2400 baud modem, the first BBSs with Mike — was before I got my first 386DX40. The 386DX at 40 megahertz was a different world: 32-bit, a hard drive, DOS and then Windows, a machine that could hold more than one thing in its head at once. It arrived in the early 1990s, and it changed what a home computer was.

But the BBS days came first. The C64 came first. The 2400 baud handshake came first. The 386DX40 was the machine that came after the lesson was already learned — that a computer is yours when you run it on your own iron, on your own line, with your own data. The 386 was faster. The Commodore was the teacher.

Calling The First Bbs

Calling the First BBSs

The first BBSs were one phone line and one computer. A sysop ran a board from a spare room, the modem answered on the second ring, and if someone was already on, you got a busy signal and tried again later. There was no web. There were boards, and the boards were everything.

You called a number, the modem shook hands, and the board answered in ASCII: a welcome screen, a menu, message bases, file areas. You read what people had left since your last call. You left a message. You downloaded a file if the sysop trusted you and the phone bill allowed it. Then you hung up and the line was free for the next caller.

FidoNet stitched the boards together. Messages hopped from board to board overnight, carried by machines calling each other when the rates were cheap. That was the network: no backbone, no cloud, just modems and sysops who kept the mail moving. darknet.ca started in that spirit — a board, a sysop, a line that answered.

Commodore 64 Days

Commodore 64 Days

My first computer was a Commodore 64. 64 kilobytes of RAM, a tape drive, a television for a monitor. It felt infinite. You turned it on and it said READY, and the cursor blinked, and the machine waited for you to tell it something.

The SID chip sang. Three voices, and every game sounded like it had a band living inside the box. The 6581, then the 8580 — the sound of the whole era, made by a chip that was designed to be a synthesizer first and a computer's voice second.

I learned to code on it. BASIC, then assembly. PEEK and POKE. The machine did what you told it, nothing more, nothing less. LOAD from tape meant waiting, watching the borders flash, listening to the tape warble. When the program ran, it was yours — you had waited for it, and you knew what every line did, because you had typed it or read it or earned it.

The Commodore taught me: own the machine, own the data, own the mind. CETI is that lesson, grown up.

Mike

Mike

I called the first BBSs with Mike, one of my sister's friends, in the mid-90s. Mike was the one who knew the numbers — which boards were up, which sysop was friendly, which number not to bother with because it was always busy. We used a Commodore 64 and a 2400 baud modem, and we took turns at the keyboard.

That is how you learned the BBS world: someone showed you. Someone who had already been there told you the number, told you what to type, told you not to tie up the phone line. Mike was that someone for me. The boards we called were the first ones I ever saw, and they are the reason darknet.ca exists — the board as a place, the sysop as a host, the call as a visit.

Salt Library

THE SALT LIBRARY — a style sampler (original text)

Mara had worked the night desk at the Salt Library for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that books exhale. Not metaphorically. On quiet nights, when the fog came off the bay and pressed its grey face against the high windows, she could hear them: a soft collective sigh from the stacks, as if ten thousand paper lungs were letting go of the day's held breath.

She shelved by intuition now. The catalog had given up on her years ago — or she on it. A patron would describe a feeling, not a title: "the one where the house is angry," "the one with the blue train," and Mara's feet would take her unerringly to the right aisle, the right shelf, the right spine. The books, she had come to understand, were not organized by author or subject. They were organized by weather. Some aisles were perpetually autumn. Others held a static, unmoving noon.

The new arrival came on a Tuesday, wrapped in oilcloth and twine, with no return address and no catalog card. It was warm. That was the first wrong thing — the parcel was warm, as if it had been sitting in sunlight, though the day outside was iron and rain. Mara carried it to the back room and set it on the sorting table, and the lamp above the table flickered once, thoughtfully, as if considering whether to stay lit.

She should have logged it. That was procedure: everything gets a card, everything gets a number, everything gets its weather assessed and recorded. Instead she untied the twine. The oilcloth fell away like a shed skin, and the book beneath was bound in something that was not leather — something smoother, with a faint nap to it, like the skin of a fruit. The title, stamped in dull gold, read: FIELD GUIDE TO VANISHED ROOMS.

Mara opened it to a random page. The text described, in precise and loving detail, the reading room she was standing in — the sorting table, the thoughtful lamp, the parcel's discarded wrapping. The last line of the page read: "She looks up now, because she has realized the book is describing this exact moment."

Mara looked up. The lamp flickered twice. In the stacks, ten thousand paper lungs drew breath all at once — and held it.
