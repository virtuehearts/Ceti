A Day on the Console

John, founder of darknet.ca — a simulated day of use, CETI as the command-and-control console.

My first computer was a Commodore 64. 64KB, a tape drive, a TV for a monitor. It felt infinite. The SID chip sang. I learned to code on it — BASIC, then assembly. PEEK and POKE. The machine did what you told it, nothing more, nothing less.

In the mid-90s I called the first BBSs with Mike, one of my sister's friends. Mike knew the numbers — which boards were up, which sysop was friendly, which number not to bother with because it was always busy. We used a Commodore 64 and a 2400 baud modem, and we took turns at the keyboard. That is how you learned the BBS world: someone showed you. All of this was before I got my first 386DX40.

Cypherpunk

The BBS days taught me the first half of the cypherpunk lesson: a board is yours when you run it on your own iron, on your own line, with your own data. The second half came from the cypherpunks — the people who argued, in the early 1990s, that privacy is not secrecy, that a free society needs anonymous transactions, and that cryptography is how ordinary people get privacy back. Eric Hughes wrote it down in A Cypherpunk's Manifesto (1993). The summary is the creed: privacy for the weak, transparency for the powerful. Read it here — it is short, and it still reads like it was written yesterday.

The cypherpunks did not just write. They built: Chaum's DigiCash (e-cash, blind signatures), Back's Hashcash (proof of work), Dai's b-money, Szabo's bit gold, e-gold. Every one of them was an attempt to answer the same question — can money exist without a bank in the middle? Each one failed or stalled, and each one taught the next one something. The culture was: write the code, run the experiment, keep the notes.

PayPal and UO Gold

We learned the same lesson the hard way, selling Ultima Online gold. Virtual goods had real value — players wanted gold, and they paid real money for it. PayPal was the bridge: a buyer sent money, we delivered gold. It worked, and it taught us the friction. A payment could be reversed. An account could be frozen. The bridge belonged to someone else, and someone else's rules decided whether your money moved.

That is the experience that makes the cypherpunk argument concrete. It is not theory when you have watched a payment reverse. The lesson we took from PayPal and UO gold: if the money moves through someone else's permission, it is not really yours. The BBS taught us to own the machine. The gold taught us to want to own the money too. CETI is the same lesson, applied to the mind: own the machine, own the data, own the mind.

The Console

Today the console is CETI. Calculator, dictionary, thesaurus, memopad, notes, weather — the desk of a sysop, in one terminal. Key authentication on the regula-core identity: a challenge, a signature, a verification. No passwords, no shared secrets. The public key is the identity; the signature is the proof.

The machine answers from memory first. It learns. It researches while I sleep. That is the Commodore spirit, grown up.
