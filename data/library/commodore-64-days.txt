Commodore 64 Days

My first computer was a Commodore 64. 64 kilobytes of RAM, a tape
drive, a television for a monitor. It felt infinite. You turned it
on and it said READY, and the cursor blinked, and the machine
waited for you to tell it something.

The SID chip sang. Three voices, and every game sounded like it
had a band living inside the box. The 6581, then the 8580 — the
sound of the whole era, made by a chip that was designed to be a
synthesizer first and a computer's voice second.

I learned to code on it. BASIC, then assembly. PEEK and POKE. The
machine did what you told it, nothing more, nothing less. LOAD
from tape meant waiting, watching the borders flash, listening to
the tape warble. When the program ran, it was yours — you had
waited for it, and you knew what every line did, because you had
typed it or read it or earned it.

The Commodore taught me: own the machine, own the data, own the
mind. CETI is that lesson, grown up.
