THE SALT LIBRARY — a style sampler (original text)

Mara had worked the night desk at the Salt Library for eleven years,
and in that time she had learned that books exhale. Not metaphorically.
On quiet nights, when the fog came off the bay and pressed its grey
face against the high windows, she could hear them: a soft collective
sigh from the stacks, as if ten thousand paper lungs were letting go
of the day's held breath.

She shelved by intuition now. The catalog had given up on her years
ago — or she on it. A patron would describe a feeling, not a title:
"the one where the house is angry," "the one with the blue train,"
and Mara's feet would take her unerringly to the right aisle, the
right shelf, the right spine. The books, she had come to understand,
were not organized by author or subject. They were organized by
weather. Some aisles were perpetually autumn. Others held a static,
unmoving noon.

The new arrival came on a Tuesday, wrapped in oilcloth and twine,
with no return address and no catalog card. It was warm. That was
the first wrong thing — the parcel was warm, as if it had been
sitting in sunlight, though the day outside was iron and rain. Mara
carried it to the back room and set it on the sorting table, and the
lamp above the table flickered once, thoughtfully, as if considering
whether to stay lit.

She should have logged it. That was procedure: everything gets a
card, everything gets a number, everything gets its weather
assessed and recorded. Instead she untied the twine. The oilcloth
fell away like a shed skin, and the book beneath was bound in
something that was not leather — something smoother, with a faint
nap to it, like the skin of a fruit. The title, stamped in dull
gold, read: FIELD GUIDE TO VANISHED ROOMS.

Mara opened it to a random page. The text described, in precise and
loving detail, the reading room she was standing in — the sorting
table, the thoughtful lamp, the parcel's discarded wrapping. The
last line of the page read: "She looks up now, because she has
realized the book is describing this exact moment."

Mara looked up. The lamp flickered twice. In the stacks, ten
thousand paper lungs drew breath all at once — and held it.
